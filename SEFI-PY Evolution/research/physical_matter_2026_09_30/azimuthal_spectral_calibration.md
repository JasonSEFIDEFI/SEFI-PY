# Azimuthal Bogoliubov sectors and symmetry calibration

**Date:** 2026-09-30  
**Status:** analytical specification for the spectral solver; no spectrum claimed  
**Depends on:** \`action_traveling_audit.md\`, \`linear_spectral_formulation.md\`  
**Baseline implementation preserved:** \`coupled-ring-axisymmetric-v0.1\`

## 1. Full angular decomposition

Restore the azimuthal dependence before linearization. Write the stationary backgrounds as

\[
\Phi_0=e^{i\Omega t}p_0(r,Z),
\qquad
\Sigma_0=e^{i\nu\gamma(t-vz)}e^{iN\varphi}s_0(r,Z),
\]

where \(p_0=u+iw\) and \(s_0\) is real.

For one azimuthal perturbation sector \(m\ge0\), use Bogoliubov pairs

\[
\delta\Phi=e^{i\Omega t}
\left[
a(r,Z)e^{\sigma T+im\varphi}
+b^*(r,Z)e^{\sigma^*T-im\varphi}
\right],
\]

\[
\delta\Sigma=e^{i\nu\gamma(t-vz)}
\left[
c(r,Z)e^{\sigma T+i(N+m)\varphi}
+d^*(r,Z)e^{\sigma^*T+i(N-m)\varphi}
\right].
\]

This form is forced by the conjugate couplings in the linearized equations. It also shows explicitly why a carrier background with winding \(N\) produces different angular operators for its two Bogoliubov components.

Define

\[
\Delta_\ell
=
\partial_r^2+\frac1r\partial_r+\partial_Z^2-\frac{\ell^2}{r^2}.
\]

Then the \(\Phi\) pair uses \(\Delta_m\) for both \(a\) and \(b\), whereas the carrier pair uses

\[
\Delta_{N+m}\quad\text{for }c,
\qquad
\Delta_{N-m}\quad\text{for }d.
\]

For the validated baseline \(N=0\), both carrier components reduce to \(\Delta_m\). This is why \(N=0\) is the appropriate first implementation target even though the general solver should retain the shifted indices.

## 2. Sector closure

The background \(\Phi_0\) is axisymmetric and the carrier has phase \(e^{iN\varphi}\). The nonlinear first variations contain

\[
p_0^*\delta\Phi+p_0\delta\Phi^*
\]

and the corresponding carrier amplitude combinations. Multiplication by the background carrier phase shifts the \(\Sigma\) perturbations exactly to \(N\pm m\). Therefore the four amplitudes \((a,b,c,d)\) form a closed linear sector for each integer \(m\). Distinct \(m\) sectors do not mix in the continuum axisymmetric background problem.

## 3. Mandatory neutral modes

Before interpreting any computed unstable eigenvalue, the discretization must reproduce the neutral modes required by continuous symmetries.

### Carrier phase mode: m=0

The exact global symmetry \(\Sigma\mapsto e^{i\alpha}\Sigma\) gives

\[
\delta\Sigma=i\Sigma_0.
\]

After removal of the common carrier phase this corresponds to a localized \(m=0\) perturbation proportional to \(is_0\). Because \(s_0=0\) at the outer boundary, the present Dirichlet carrier boundary condition preserves this symmetry. This is the strongest discrete zero-mode calibration.

### Longitudinal translation: m=0

Translation in \(z\) gives a tangent perturbation generated by the physical derivative of the full fields. In reduced coordinates this contains profile derivatives and, for the carrier, the derivative of its longitudinal phase. Consequently the exact physical translation generator must be constructed from the full ansatz rather than identified only with \((p_{0,Z},s_{0,Z})\). The finite box breaks translation invariance, so its eigenvalue need only converge toward zero as the domain grows.

This corrects the shorthand statement in the previous discussion that profile derivatives alone are automatically the full physical translation zero mode.

### Transverse translations: m=1

Translations in \(x\) and \(y\) of an axisymmetric ring generate \(m=1\) perturbations. For \(N=0\), these supply the two real transverse translation directions. The finite cylindrical boundary breaks transverse translation symmetry, so the corresponding eigenvalues should approach zero with increasing domain rather than be required to vanish at fixed \(L\).

### Background Phi phase

The formal global \(\Phi\) phase variation does not decay because \(|p_0|\to1\). Under the fixed asymptotic phase used by the current numerical problem it is not an ordinary localized eigenmode and must not be used as a finite-box zero-mode requirement.

## 4. Spectral acceptance protocol

The first solver should target the validated \(N=0,\lambda=25\) branch and independently compute sectors \(m=0,1,2,\ldots\). Before an eigenvalue with positive real part is accepted as physical evidence of instability:

1. the carrier phase mode must be recovered near zero at discretization accuracy;
2. translation-related modes must move toward zero under increasing-domain refinement;
3. the candidate unstable eigenvalue must converge under independent grid refinement;
4. the candidate must persist under increasing-domain refinement;
5. the eigenfunction must be resolved and not concentrated at the artificial outer boundary;
6. the result must be reproduced without imposing the background's axial parity on arbitrary perturbations.

A persistent \(\operatorname{Re}\sigma>0\) satisfying these checks falsifies spectral stability of that branch in the corresponding sector. Failure to find one establishes only the searched numerical spectral result, not nonlinear stability.

## 5. Implementation consequence

The stability solver should use the full \(Z\in[-L,L]\) domain rather than inherit the profile solver's real-even/imaginary-odd half-domain reduction. The latter is appropriate for constructing the symmetric stationary profile but can project out perturbations of opposite parity.

The existing \`ring_operator.py\` and \`physics.py\` remain reference implementations for the stationary profile and time evolution. They are not to be silently repurposed as the spectral operator.

## 6. Immediate next calculation

Construct the \(N=0\) full-domain discrete quadratic operator, verify the carrier-phase null vector directly, convert it to first-order form, and compute a small set of eigenvalues nearest the imaginary axis for \(m=0\) and \(m=1\). Only after those calibration modes behave correctly should the search be extended to unstable eigenvalues and higher \(m\).
